The world is warming, and faster than it used to

By Samuel Gunawan

Every year since 1977 has been warmer than the 1951–1980 global average. Anyone born since then has never lived through a year cooler than the average their grandparents grew up with. This page follows that change from 1880 to 2025, around the world and in Australia.

How to read these charts

Most charts show a temperature anomaly: the difference from a long-term average. An anomaly of +1°C means one degree warmer than that average. Unless a chart says otherwise, the average is for 1951–1980, so 0°C is "normal for the mid-20th century".

Red means warmer than the average, blue cooler and white no change. Grey means no data. Purple shows how fast warming is changing, not temperature itself. Hover over any chart for exact values.

Part 1 · The long view

The world is about 1.3°C warmer than a century ago

Until the 1970s, global temperature rose and fell by a few tenths of a degree, including a temporary warm spell around the 1940s. From the late 1970s it began a steady climb, and the 10-year average has risen by about 0.2°C every decade since.

The heatmap below the line splits each year into its 12 months. The change from blue to red happens in every month at about the same time, so the warming covers the whole year, not just summer.

Part 2 · The pace of change

Warming has sped up since 1980

Between 1880 and 1939 the world warmed by about 0.01°C per decade. Over 1980–2025 the rate was 0.21°C per decade, and since 2010 it has been about 0.36°C. Since the 1960s, every decade has been warmer than the one before.

The ranking chart shows the ten warmest years on record, re-ranked every five years. As each new record arrives, older years drop down and out. By 2025, all ten of the warmest years fall between 2015 and 2025.

Part 3 · Around the world

Every country is warmer, and the far north is warming fastest

The first map shows how much warmer each country was in a chosen year than its own 1951–1980 average. In 2025, all 194 countries were warmer; Australia by 0.83°C and Malaysia by 1.28°C. Each colour is a national average, so it hides differences within large countries such as Australia.

The second map shows something different: how fast each 5° square of land and ocean has warmed since 1980. The Arctic (north of 60°N) is warming at about 0.56°C per decade, almost three times the global 0.21°C, while parts of the Southern Ocean have barely changed.

Part 4 · Country by country

Warming has sped up in most countries

The circles compare each country's warming rate in 1980–2025 with its rate in 1950–1979. A bigger circle means a bigger change. Warming sped up in 168 of 194 countries, most of all in Europe and the Middle East. Australia went from a slight cooling (−0.20°C per decade) to warming (+0.13°C per decade).

The two charts below add detail. The jitter plot places every country in every decade, so you can watch the whole group move upward. The dumbbell chart compares the two rates for 19 countries; India and New Zealand are the only two of these that slowed slightly.

Part 5 · Australia

Australia: spring and summer have warmed most

Comparing 2000–2025 with 1951–1980, Australian spring (September to November) warmed by 0.66°C and summer (December to February) by 0.54°C. Winter warmed by 0.40°C and autumn by only 0.18°C.

The circular chart shows each month, decade by decade. The outer ring (the 2020s) is the reddest: August and September in the 2020s averaged 1.2 to 1.3°C above their 1951–1980 level. Australia's warmest year in this data was 2019.

Part 6 · Day and night

Nights warmed first, then days caught up

Over global land, night-time lows started rising earlier than daytime highs. By the 1980s, nights were 0.52°C warmer than in the 1950s, while days were only 0.22°C warmer.

Daytime highs then rose faster, and by 2010–2015 both were about 1.0°C above their 1950s level. Warmer nights matter for health, because the body has less chance to recover after a hot day. This dataset ends in 2015.

Conclusion

What the data shows

The world is about 1.3°C warmer than in the early 1900s, and most of that rise has happened since 1980. Separate records agree: NASA's global series matches both the Copernicus ERA5 data and Berkeley Earth's grid closely, with a correlation of 0.99 in each case.

Warming has also sped up: close to zero before 1940, about 0.2°C per decade after 1980, and higher again since 2010. Australia has warmed less than the global average, but its springs and summers have warmed noticeably, and four of its five warmest years have come since 2013.
