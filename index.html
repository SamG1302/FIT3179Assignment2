The world is warming, and faster than it used to

By Samuel Gunawan

Every year since 1977 has been warmer than the 1951–1980 global average. Anyone born since then has never lived through a year cooler than the average their grandparents grew up with. This page follows that change from 1880 to 2025, both around the world and here in Australia.

Most charts below measure temperature as an anomaly: how far it sits above or below the 1951–1980 average. So 0°C means "normal for the mid-20th century", and +1°C means one degree warmer. Hover over any chart to see the exact number behind each mark.

A century on, the world is 1.3°C warmer

Until the 1970s, global temperature rose and fell by a few tenths of a degree, including a temporary warm spell around the 1940s. Pollution from industry in the 1950s–70s reflected some sunlight and held the rise back.1,3 From the late 1970s temperature began a steady climb, and the 10-year average has risen by about 0.2°C every decade since.

The long climb comes mainly from greenhouse gases.1 Since the Industrial Revolution, burning coal, oil and gas has added carbon dioxide to the air, where it traps heat.2 Shorter spikes and dips come from natural events, such as El Niño years in the Pacific4 and large volcanic eruptions.5

Warming has sped up since 1980

A warming rate in °C per decade is how much warmer the world gets every ten years. Between 1880 and 1939 it was about 0.01°C per decade. Over 1980–2025 the rate was 0.21°C per decade, and since 2010 it has been about 0.36°C. Since the 1960s, every decade has been warmer than the one before.

The second chart marks every year that set a new global record. From 1944 to 1980 there were none. Since 1980 there have been 14, and 2023 and 2024 each beat the old record by more than 0.1°C.

Every country is warmer, the Arctic first

The first map shows how much warmer each country was in a chosen year than its own 1951–1980 average. In 2025, all 194 countries were warmer; Australia by 0.83°C and Malaysia by 1.28°C. Each colour is a national average, so it hides differences within large countries such as Australia.

The second map asks a different question: when did each 5° square of land and ocean pass +1°C and stay above it? The Arctic got there first, in the 1990s and 2000s. Most land followed in the 2010s and 2020s, but much of the tropical ocean has not reached +1°C yet. In Australia, 11 of 27 land squares have, most of them only since 2020.

Warming has sped up in most countries

The circles compare each country's warming rate in 1980–2025 with its rate in 1950–1979. A bigger circle means a bigger change. Warming sped up in 168 of 194 countries, most of all in Europe and the Middle East. Australia went from a slight cooling (−0.20°C per decade) to warming (+0.13°C per decade).

In the 1940s, only about one in three countries was warmer than its 1951–1980 average. Since the 2000s, every country has been. Of 19 major countries compared side by side, 17 are warming faster than before 1980; only India and New Zealand slowed slightly.

Our World in Data (ERA5)

Australia: warmer springs and summers

Comparing 2000–2025 with 1951–1980, Australian spring (September to November) warmed by 0.66°C and summer (December to February) by 0.54°C. Winter warmed by 0.40°C and autumn by only 0.18°C.

Month by month, the 2020s stand out as the warmest decade: August and September in the 2020s averaged 1.2 to 1.3°C above their 1951–1980 level. Australia's warmest year in this data was 2019.

Our World in Data (ERA5)

Nights warmed first, then days caught up

Over global land, night-time lows started rising earlier than daytime highs. By the 1980s, nights were 0.52°C warmer than in the 1950s, while days had warmed by only 0.22°C.

Daytime highs then rose faster, and by 2010–2015 both were about 1.0°C above their 1950s level. Warmer nights matter for health: heat at night disturbs sleep, and hot nights are linked to more deaths.6

What the data shows

The world is about 1.3°C warmer than in the early 1900s, and most of that rise has happened since 1980. Separate records agree: NASA's global series matches both the Copernicus ERA5 data and Berkeley Earth's grid closely, with a correlation of 0.99 in each case.

Warming has also sped up: close to zero before 1940, about +0.2°C per decade after 1980, and +0.36°C per decade since 2010. Australia has warmed less than the global average, but its springs and summers have warmed noticeably, and four of its five warmest years on record have all come since 2013.
