The world is warming, and faster than it used to

By Samuel Gunawan

Every year since 1977 has been warmer than the 1951–1980 global average. Anyone born since then has never lived through a year cooler than the average their grandparents grew up with. This page follows that change from 1880 to 2025, around the world and in Australia.

How to read these charts

Most charts show a temperature anomaly: the difference from a long-term average. An anomaly of +1°C means one degree warmer than that average. Unless a chart says otherwise, the average is for 1951–1980, so 0°C is "normal for the mid-20th century".

Red means warmer than the average and blue means cooler. Grey marks places or periods with no data. Hover over any chart for exact values.

Part 1 · The long view

The world is about 1.3°C warmer than a century ago

Until the 1970s, global temperature rose and fell by a few tenths of a degree, including a temporary warm spell around the 1940s. From the late 1970s it began a steady climb, and the 10-year average has risen by about 0.2°C every decade since. The 2016–2025 average was 1.3°C above the 1901–1930 average.

The heatmap below the line breaks each year into its 12 months. The shift from blue to red happens in every month at about the same time, so the change covers the whole year.

Part 2 · The pace of change

Warming has sped up since 1980

Between 1880 and 1939 the world warmed by about 0.01°C per decade. Over 1980–2025 the rate was 0.21°C per decade, and since 2010 it has been about 0.36°C. The left chart shows this decade by decade: since the 1960s, each decade has been warmer than the one before.

The right chart tracks the ten warmest years on record as the record grew. Older years drop out as newer, hotter ones arrive. By 2025, every year in the top ten falls between 2015 and 2025.

Part 3 · Around the world

Every country is warmer, and the far north most of all

All 194 countries in the data were warmer in 1996–2025 than in 1951–1980. The typical country warmed by about 1.1°C. Australia warmed by 0.43°C, less than most, while Malaysia warmed by 1.07°C, close to the typical amount.

Countries are uneven units, so the second map uses equal 5° grid squares that also cover the oceans. It shows the Arctic (north of 60°N) warming at about 0.56°C per decade since 1980, almost three times the global rate of 0.21°C.

Part 4 · Country by country

Most countries are warming faster than before

The map compares each country's warming rate since 1980 with its rate in 1950–1979. In 168 of 194 countries the rate has increased. Australia changed from a slight cooling of 0.20°C per decade to a warming of 0.13°C per decade.

The two charts below give the detail. The jitter plot places every country in every decade, so you can see the whole spread move upward. The dumbbell chart compares the two rates for 19 countries. Russia's rate rose the most of these; India and New Zealand are the two that slowed slightly.

Part 5 · Australia

Australia: spring and summer have warmed most

Comparing 2000–2025 with 1951–1980, Australian spring (September to November) warmed by 0.66°C and summer (December to February) by 0.54°C. Winter warmed by 0.40°C and autumn by 0.18°C, so the warming is uneven across the year.

The circular chart shows the same months decade by decade, with the 1940s at the centre and the 2020s on the outside edge. The outer ring is the reddest, and August and September in the 2020s have averaged 1.2 to 1.3°C above their 1951–1980 level. Australia's warmest year in this data was 2019.

Part 6 · Day and night

Nights warmed first, then days caught up

Over global land, night-time lows started rising earlier than daytime highs. By the 1980s, nights were 0.52°C warmer than in the 1950s while days were only 0.22°C warmer. Daytime highs then rose faster, and by 2010–2015 both were about 1.0°C above their 1950s level. Warmer nights matter for health, because they give the body less chance to recover after a hot day. Conclusion

What the data shows

The world is about 1.3°C warmer than in the early 1900s, and most of that rise has happened since 1980. Separate records agree on this: NASA's global series matches both the Copernicus ERA5 data and Berkeley Earth's grid closely (a correlation of 0.99 in each case).

The rate of warming has also increased. It was close to zero before 1940, about 0.2°C per decade after 1980, and higher again since 2010. Most countries show the same increase, and the Arctic is warming fastest.

Australia has warmed less than the global average, but its springs and summers have warmed noticeably, and four of its five warmest years have come since 2013. If the post-1980 rate continues, each coming decade will add roughly another 0.2°C to the global figure.
